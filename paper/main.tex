\begin{abstract}
This is a single-application \emph{case study}. We report what a stack of
lightweight, testable checks does and does not establish for one secure-multiparty
computation (MPC) application: the deterministic-query fragment of the
knowledge-threshold belief computation of Mardziel, Hicks, Katz and Srivatsa (PLAS
2012), implemented in MP-SPDZ (three-party replicated secret sharing, Rep3). The
checks are a separately implemented plaintext \emph{pseudo-oracle} derived from the
same paper, an anti-echo interface that keeps expected outputs out of the circuit,
a strict runtime transcript check, and recomputed, hash-bound evidence. On the
tested inputs these provide useful, coverage-bounded assurance; we are careful to
state each guarantee operationally rather than as formal soundness. We then run an
\emph{adaptive, retrospectively discovered} mutation sequence against a static
delivery linter under a precisely bounded build-time adversary that controls the
application source but not the oracle, checker, policy, CI workflow, evidence
validator, or pinned compiler. The sequence exposes where opcode-level delivery
linting fails: a masked comparison-open and a raw secret-reveal are the same
opcode, and the MP-SPDZ \calltape/\callarg{} register channel that a leak could use
is the same channel the honest comparison subtapes use, so it cannot be closed by
an opcode/channel blocklist without rejecting the honest build. We do not claim
this principle is new --- that leakage is a dataflow rather than opcode-identity
property is established for constant-time and smart-contract bytecode analysis; our
contribution is the MPC-application instantiation, the pinned-backend mechanism, and
a reproducible mutation study. We archive the decisive mutations and a
machine-readable detection matrix, and we are explicit about adaptive overfitting,
the trusted-computing-base boundary, and the absence of independent validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An MPC framework such as MP-SPDZ~\cite{keller2020mpspdz} compiles a program into a
protocol that several parties execute so each learns only its prescribed output.
Building a correct \emph{application} on top of such a framework is separate from the
framework's own security argument: an application author can, by accident or intent,
write source that reveals a value the functionality was meant to protect. When such
an application is wired into continuous integration (CI), a green check is easy to
mistake for evidence that the privacy property holds. This paper is a case study of
what that check can and cannot establish.

We are explicit about what this paper is \emph{not}. It is not ``the first
implementation of PLAS belief tracking,'' and makes no such claim: earlier
adversarial review on this project established, correctly, that implementing and
timing a 2012 construction is not by itself a research contribution~\repo{docs/review-log.md
R-10}. It is also not a new analysis \emph{technique}: the principle that
information-flow safety depends on dataflow and semantics rather than instruction
names is prior art (\S\ref{sec:related}). We frame the work as an
\emph{experience report / case study} whose value is the concrete instantiation, the
reproducible mutation study, and the honest accounting of the assurance boundary.

\paragraph{A narrowed thesis.} On one MP-SPDZ application, separately implemented
functional pseudo-oracles, anti-echo interfaces, transcript checks, and recomputed
evidence provide useful coverage-bounded assurance; an adaptive mutation sequence
exposes where opcode-identity delivery linting fails, including the MP-SPDZ
\calltape/\callarg{} path, under a precisely bounded build-time adversary.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
\item An \textbf{application} of established conformance / pseudo-oracle practice to
an MPC application (\S\ref{sec:method}), with the anti-echo interface and the
recomputed evidence discipline as the local delta.
\item A \textbf{reproducible case-study artifact} for the deterministic-query
fragment of \thsmc{} (\S\ref{sec:results}): a fixture and a bounded 228-case
adversarial matrix checked against the pseudo-oracle, and five committed executable
negative controls, against a pinned backend.
\item A \textbf{reproducible adversarial mutation study} (\S\ref{sec:mutation}):
one motivating transcript failure, six compiled-delivery mutations, and one semantic
mutation, with an archived corpus and a machine-readable detection matrix, showing
where opcode-level linting fails and \emph{at what exact strength}.
\item A statement of the \textbf{assurance boundary and hand-off}
(\S\ref{sec:boundary}): the operational property the checks demonstrate, and the two
protocol-level properties a real non-leakage guarantee would additionally need.
\end{enumerate}
We report the three-way adversarial review process (\S\ref{sec:experience}) as an
\emph{experience lesson}, not a scientific contribution: we have no comparator or
controlled evaluation and do not claim it outperforms a single review pass.

\section{Background and two adversaries}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{The functionality (deterministic-query fragment).} Following PLAS
2012~\cite{plas2012}, $N$ parties hold secrets in a finite domain $D$; each observer
$j$ holds a belief over the others' secrets. For a query $Q$, the mechanism checks,
per recipient, whether releasing $Q$'s output would let any protected party's
posterior exceed a public threshold, quantifying over \emph{every possible output}
so the accept/reject decision is simulatable; on accept the recipient privately
learns the output, on reject a masked constant, and no party may learn another's
verdict~\repo{conformance/CONTRACT.md}. We study deterministic, total, public
queries; two instantiate it: \code{p1\_is\_max} and \code{sum\_even}.

\paragraph{Backend.} Circuits are compiled and run with MP-SPDZ pinned at
\code{9d809599}, backend \code{replicated-ring-party.x}: three-party replicated
secret sharing over $\Ztfour$, semi-honest, honest majority. The compiler emits a
manifest of \emph{tapes} (a main tape plus comparison subtapes). Two facts about
this compiled form recur. First, a public reveal and a legitimate masked
comparison-open are the \emph{same} opcode: MP-SPDZ ``uses a single instruction for
opening''~\cite{keller2020mpspdz}, and the correctness-check flag does not change
this~\repo{conformance/delivery\_inspect.py}. Second, a value in one tape reaches
another only through memory or through register arguments passed by \calltape{}
(opcode \code{0xec}) and received by \callarg{} (opcode \code{0xed}); a cross-tape
register reference is a compiler error~\repo{MP-SPDZ docs: instructions.html}.

\paragraph{Two separate adversaries.} We keep two adversaries distinct throughout;
conflating them is what makes ``source-controlling author'' sound vacuous.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[Runtime Rep3 adversary.] At most one semi-honest corrupted party among the
three; honest majority; no collusion; authenticated encrypted channels; process
isolation (stated, \emph{not} enforced by the single-host test harness). This is the
adversary a \emph{protocol-level} privacy argument would target. Our functional and
linting evidence does \emph{not} address it; that hand-off is \S\ref{sec:boundary}.
\item[Build-time mutation adversary.] Controls the target \code{.mpc} application
source, \emph{and only that}. The pseudo-oracle, the linter/checker, the policy
(thresholds, expected delivery), the CI workflow, the evidence validator, and the
\emph{pinned} MP-SPDZ compiler are all in the trusted computing base (TCB) and are
\emph{not} under adversary control. The mutation study (\S\ref{sec:mutation}) is
conducted against this adversary. Were the TCB itself adversarial, every automated
check would be trivially void; stating the boundary is what makes the study
meaningful.
\end{description}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} draws this boundary. We make no claim under collusion, a
malicious party, unencrypted channels, or a shared host, and no performance claim.

\section{Method: conformance checks for one MPC application}
\label{sec:method}

Each element exists to defeat a specific way a conformance check can be vacuous. We
state what each element \emph{operationally} provides, not that it is ``sound.''

\paragraph{A separately implemented pseudo-oracle.} The plaintext oracle is written
from the paper, separately from the circuit and from the project's earlier
exact-rational reference model, and transcribes the all-possible-outputs check
directly~\repo{conformance/oracle.py}. It is a \emph{pseudo-oracle}
(a separate implementation of the same specification), \emph{not} an independently
validated ground truth: it shares the specification with the circuit, so a
\emph{correlated} misreading of PLAS could make both agree on the wrong behaviour.
It did carry one fatal transcription error (conditioning on the actual output only)
until review caught it~\repo{docs/review-log.md R-13}. We state this shared-spec /
correlated-error risk wherever we rely on oracle agreement (\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{An anti-echo interface.} Before any circuit was written, the interface
was fixed so that expected outputs and expected post-state \emph{never enter the
circuit} --- neither compiled in nor as runtime inputs; they live only in the
external harness, obtained from the pseudo-oracle~\repo{conformance/INTERFACE.md;
R-17,R-18}. Circuit inputs are exactly the public parameters and the secret-shared
secrets and beliefs, and the harness runs at least one state beyond the fixture, so
a constant-output circuit cannot pass by accident. Beliefs travel as dense
unnormalized non-negative integer weights; the threshold check avoids division
($b\,M \le a\,Z$). Because MP-SPDZ comparison is signed, the no-wraparound
requirement is $B < 2^{k-1}$ (not the naive $2^k > B$), which we pin with a
counterexample~\repo{circuit\_spec.py; R-22}.

\paragraph{A strict transcript check.} A correct party's captured stdout must be
exactly its two own records; any foreign, duplicate, unknown, missing, or extra line
makes the check \emph{fail closed}~\repo{conformance/private\_run.py}. This is an
operational property over the enumerated transcript cases on the observed stdout
channel; it observes selected output channels and does not establish absence of
every possible leak.

\paragraph{Recomputed, hash-bound evidence.} Each run retains a typed record; in CI
the validator requires each record's source hash and delivery signature to equal
values recomputed from the checked-out source and a fresh compile, and binds each
record to a canonical case via a recomputed input hash and pseudo-oracle
verdict~\repo{conformance/validate\_evidence.py; R-34,R-37,R-43}. This establishes
\emph{self-consistency and provenance} of the evidence \emph{under the trusted CI
workflow}; it is not integrity against an adversary who can alter source, checker,
workflow, and evidence together (that adversary is outside the TCB of
\S\ref{sec:background}). A \code{pipefail} canary prevents a failing suite from
passing through a logging pipe~\repo{R-31}.

\subsection{Artifact and results}
\label{sec:results}
Foregrounding properties and inputs rather than counts: on the fixture (two
invocations: one all-accept, one with a per-recipient reject and belief
preservation) plus two further valid states, the circuit reproduces the
pseudo-oracle's visible outputs, payloads, and reconstructed weights. A
\emph{bounded, constructed} adversarial matrix of 228 cases (216 single-invocation +
12 carried; all 27 secrets $\times$ two queries $\times$ uniform / non-uniform /
per-party-scaled integer weights near the bit bound) all match the
pseudo-oracle~\repo{coverage.py; results-coverage.txt}. This is a constructed test
matrix, \emph{not} general coverage. The conformance package holds 92 tests --- 46
functional and 46 delivery-gate/linter tests --- and 9 reference tests, all green;
test counts are not themselves a result. Five committed executable negative controls
are rejected by the linter (\S\ref{sec:mutation}). No timing is reported: the
repository's older timings measure a different, non-conforming circuit and must not
be cited~\repo{README; docs/gap.md}.

\section{The adversarial mutation study}
\label{sec:mutation}

Functional conformance says nothing about \emph{how} a value is delivered: a circuit
can compute correct verdicts and broadcast them. We therefore built a static
compiled-delivery linter and subjected it to an \emph{adaptive} sequence of source
mutations by the build-time adversary. We are explicit that this is a
\emph{retrospective, adaptively discovered} sequence, not a complete or
independently designed taxonomy: the checker was repeatedly hardened against the same
mutations later used as evidence (adaptive overfitting), and there are no held-out
mutations from which to estimate detector effectiveness (\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{Accounting.} One motivating transcript failure (\textbf{B0}); six
compiled-delivery mutations (\textbf{B1--B6}); one semantic mutation (\textbf{S1}).
B0: changing \code{reveal\_to(j)} to a public \code{reveal()} while keeping the
per-party print compiles to a public open of every verdict, yet a stdout-only
checker reported success~\repo{R-41}. That motivated inspecting the \emph{compiled}
delivery. B1--B5 then each defeated the then-current linter and were closed by
adding a rule (separate-tape reveal; name-spoofed subtape; \code{reveal(False)} ---
still a public open; the same injected into an existing subtape body via scalar
memory; and via vectorized memory)~\repo{review-log R-42..R-46}. The clean build
stores nothing in its main tape and touches no memory in its subtapes, so forbidding
memory is a restriction with no false positives on the honest build.

\subsection{B6, at its exact strength}
\label{sec:b6}

We scope B6 precisely, because it is easy to overstate. We reconstructed a mutation
that routes party~1's verdict through the \calltape/\callarg{} register channel (a
\code{@function\_tape} that receives the verdict as a register argument and opens it).
Compiled against the pinned backend and run through the final linter, this mutation
is \textbf{REJECTED}: the added tape breaks the pinned comparison-subtape multiset,
and MP-SPDZ's call frame also spills through memory, tripping the memory rules. So
\textbf{B6 is not a demonstrated bypass of the final linter.}

Its content is narrower and precise. The \emph{honest} build passes secret
comparison operands across the tape boundary via \calltape{} (in main; e.g.
\code{call\_tape 12, 1, ci0, \dots s202(81), s0(81) \dots}) received by \callarg{} in
each of the three comparison subtapes --- no memory (reproducible;
\code{paper/mutations/}). Therefore a blocklist rule that forbade cross-tape
register-argument transfer of secrets, the way rules against memory close the memory
channel, would \textbf{reject the honest build}. The \callarg{} channel
\emph{cannot be closed by an opcode/channel blocklist} without rejecting the honest
build. This is \emph{not} a claim that no stronger analysis can distinguish
legitimate from illegitimate flows: an operand-sensitive type system, a verified IR,
a dataflow analysis, or a policy-bound call-site analysis could. Combined with the
opcode-identity fact (a masked open and a raw reveal are the same instruction), the
lesson is that a blocklist over opcodes and channels is the wrong tool; a sound
checker needs operand-sensitive dataflow (\S\ref{sec:related}). We deliberately do
\emph{not} claim that memory and register arguments are the \emph{only} cross-tape
channels; the demonstrated \callarg{} path is sufficient for this scoped conclusion.

\subsection{S1: correct recipient, wrong value}
\label{sec:s1}
On a different axis, a mutation that drops the reject-mask (delivers the true output
even to rejected recipients) \textbf{passes} every delivery-linter rule --- the
delivery structure is intact --- yet \textbf{fails} the pseudo-oracle: on
$\text{secrets}=(0,0,0)$, \code{p1\_is\_max}, the true output is $1$ and all parties
reject (oracle payload $0$), but S1 delivers $1$ to all three
(\code{paper/mutations/threshold\_smc\_wrongsem.mpc}). Delivery-linting checks
\emph{how} values are delivered, not \emph{which}; only the functional pseudo-oracle,
bounded by test coverage, constrains that.

\section{The assurance boundary}
\label{sec:boundary}

Separating three levels keeps the claims honest.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{General prior principle (not novel here):} information-flow safety
depends on dataflow and semantics, not opcode names. Established for constant-time
and smart-contract bytecode (\S\ref{sec:related}).
\item \textbf{Case-study lesson (transferable, not empirically validated across
applications):} adaptive linting of a security property can produce false
confidence, and such tooling should print its assurance boundary; ours does~\repo{delivery\_inspect.py
banner}.
\item \textbf{Pinned-backend finding (backend/version specific):} MP-SPDZ's shared
open instruction and the \calltape/\callarg{} path at \code{9d809599}, and the
consequence that the delivery linter's blocklist strategy cannot be extended to
soundness.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What would make it a real guarantee.} Two protocol-level properties,
neither supplied by our checks: (i) \emph{privacy of the executed circuit} --- a
Rep3 simulation argument (a human MPC specialist) or a formally-verified,
dataflow-checked comparison primitive; and (ii) \emph{semantic source-to-spec
binding} --- that the circuit computes the intended function, not merely one that
agrees with the pseudo-oracle on tested cases (S1 shows why privacy of delivery is
insufficient without it). A Rep3 proof establishes (i) and not (ii); our checks give
coverage-bounded evidence toward (ii) and nothing toward (i).

\section{The adversarial review process (experience)}
\label{sec:experience}

The results were produced by a three-party loop --- direction, implementation, and
adversarial review --- with a shared repository as the only mailbox~\repo{docs/workflow.md},
and an append-only 47-entry review log. We report this as an \emph{experience
lesson}, not a scientific result: we have no single-pass comparator, no ablation, and
no independent replication, so we cannot claim reproduce-before-fix review
\emph{outperforms} a single pass. Descriptively, iterative adversarial review
surfaced a fatal specification error (\S\ref{sec:method}) and the entire mutation
sequence, and it recorded its own missteps (a soundness premise asserted in one round
was refuted in the next). Whether this generalizes is unestablished.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Novelty statements below rest on a bounded, documented search~\repo{paper/LITERATURE\_REVIEW.md};
they are ``to the best of a bounded search,'' not absolute (\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{Leakage is dataflow, not opcode identity (the prior principle).}
ct-verif reduces constant-time to a 2-safety property on
LLVM~IR~\cite{ctverif}; Binsec/Rel decides constant-time \emph{relationally at the
binary/opcode level} on operand provenance, showing compiler backends inject leaks
invisible to instruction-name checks~\cite{binsecrel}; Securify decides EVM-bytecode
security on inferred \emph{dataflow} facts rather than syntactic
patterns~\cite{securify}. Our \S\ref{sec:b6} instantiates this principle in the
MPC-application setting; we claim the instantiation and the pinned-backend mechanism,
not the principle.

\paragraph{Language-based security for MPC (the positive dual).} Kerschbaum's
information-flow type system certifies non-leakage of a mixed-protocol secure
computation at the \emph{source} level with a trusted compiler~\cite{kerschbaum}.
Closest to our finding, Skalka and Near give a \emph{sound} static security-type
system for a purpose-built low-level MPC IR (Overture/Prelude), proving information
is released only through explicit declassifications~\cite{skalkanear,skalkanear2}.
It is the positive dual of our negative case-study observation: it is sound
\emph{because} its IR makes declassification syntactically distinct --- the property
MP-SPDZ bytecode lacks --- and it assumes a corrupt \emph{party}, not a malicious
\emph{author}. Verified MPC stacks and mechanized simulation proofs (Wys$^\star$,
verified SFE, EasyUC, replicated-secret-sharing proofs) are the protocol-level
targets our hand-off (\S\ref{sec:boundary}) defers to~\repo{LITERATURE\_REVIEW.md
\S3}.

\paragraph{Conformance / differential testing and negative controls.} Independent
and pseudo-oracle testing is standard~\cite{oracleproblem}; Wycheproof applies
reference-oracle conformance to crypto \emph{primitives} (feeding expected outputs,
the opposite of our anti-echo design)~\repo{LITERATURE\_REVIEW.md \S4}; MASC evaluates
crypto-misuse detectors with planted leaky mutants~\cite{masc}, the closest
precedent for negative controls, but as an offline third-party-detector benchmark
rather than committed CI controls. Reproducible Builds~\cite{reprobuilds} and
in-toto/SLSA are the evidence-discipline neighbours; ours recomputes per-case,
oracle-bound evidence rather than attesting whole artifacts.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Build-time adversary / TCB.} All results assume the TCB of
\S\ref{sec:background} (oracle, checker, policy, CI, validator, pinned compiler
trusted); a repository-controlling adversary voids them. Workflow hashes establish
\emph{self-consistency}, not authenticity against such an adversary.
\item \textbf{Correlated authorship and AI assistance.} Circuit, pseudo-oracle,
tests, review synthesis, and manuscript were produced within one project and
substantially with AI assistance; model agreement is not independent validation.
\item \textbf{Adaptive overfitting.} The linter was hardened against the same
mutations later used as evidence; there are no held-out mutations, so we do not
estimate detector effectiveness.
\item \textbf{Pseudo-oracle pseudo-independence.} The oracle shares PLAS with the
circuit; a correlated spec misreading is possible. A cross-check against the
project's exact reference model is limited because that model implements a
\emph{different} (single-observer, stateless) functionality~\repo{docs/review-log.md
R-11}; we therefore state the risk rather than claim it eliminated.
\item \textbf{Bounded literature search.} IACR ePrint full text and some indexes were
not reachable; novelty is claimed only to the best of a documented bounded
search~\repo{LITERATURE\_REVIEW.md \S11-12}.
\item \textbf{No independent validation.} No human MPC specialist has signed off and
there is no external replication.
\item \textbf{Construct / external validity.} ``Conformance'' means agreement with a
pseudo-oracle on tested inputs. Results are for one deterministic-query
functionality, $N{=}3$, $D{=}\{0,1,2\}$, one backend, and a single-host harness that
is not an adversarially isolated deployment; we do not imply an evaluated methodology
across MPC applications plural.
\item \textbf{No performance or simulation-security claim}; the boundary result is a
case-study observation, not a theorem.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:future}
Persistent secret-shared state across invocations ($\Sigma_T$) is unimplemented and
out of scope. The experiment most likely to change a conclusion is a genuine
\emph{operand-sensitive dataflow} delivery checker: if it distinguishes the honest
\callarg{} flow from a leaking one, it changes the boundary; if it fails, it shows
precisely where dataflow is required. A general impossibility statement for
dataflow-free syntactic checkers is plausible but, as we found, much of its natural
formulation is definitional and it requires an operational leak definition and a
proven safe/leaky pair; we leave it as a future-work sketch rather than a theorem.
Cross-application and cross-backend replication would move the case-study lesson
toward an evaluated one.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On one MP-SPDZ application, a separately implemented pseudo-oracle, an anti-echo
interface, a strict transcript check, and recomputed evidence give real,
coverage-bounded, operationally-stated assurance and catch a family of gross delivery
mistakes --- and they have a sharp, nameable boundary. Opcode-level delivery linting
cannot be made sound against a source-controlling author, because a masked open and a
raw reveal are the same instruction and the register channel a leak would use is the
one the honest code uses; a sound checker needs operand-sensitive dataflow, and a
privacy guarantee needs a protocol-level proof. Naming that boundary --- and printing
it next to the green check --- is the case study's point.
